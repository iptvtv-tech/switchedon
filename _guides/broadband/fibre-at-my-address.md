Enter your Eircode or address into ComReg's free Broadband Checker. It shows each fixed broadband network that's available or planned at that address, the type of broadband (for example, fibre) and its speed, and the providers that may sell it.
{: .answer}

If your home is in the National Broadband Plan area, National Broadband Ireland's map gives an estimated date for your connection. NBI doesn't sell broadband to homes, so you order from a provider.
{: .answer}

## Check what reaches your address

What you can get depends on where you live, the technology available and which providers sell in your area.

Enter your Eircode or address into ComReg's [Broadband Checker](https://www.comreg.ie/broadbandchecker). If broadband is available or planned, you'll see a table with the network operator, the type of broadband and its speed, and a list of providers that may sell broadband on that network. The checker covers four networks: NBI, open eir, SIRO and Virgin Media.

It has limits. Its data comes from the network operators and is updated quarterly in arrears, and it may show services you can't buy yet. ComReg says the results aren't a guarantee of service, so check with the network operator or a provider for your address.

## Fibre, part fibre and the rest

| Type | How it reaches you | Typical top speed (ComReg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fibre (full fibre, FTTP or FTTH) | Fibre-optic cable all the way to your home | Up to 2Gbps |
| Part fibre (FTTC) | Fibre to the street cabinet, then copper or cable | Up to 1Gbps, depending on your distance from the cabinet |
| Cable | A cable TV network, usually part fibre | Up to 1Gbps |
| Copper | The traditional telephone line | Slower than fibre, part fibre or cable |

Copper networks are based on older technology, and full fibre is being rolled out to more of the country.

Other types are fixed wireless, which uses radio signals from a local base station; satellite, which uses a dish on your property; and mobile broadband, which uses the same 4G or 5G signals as a phone, though building materials and insulation may reduce the signal indoors.

Whichever type you choose, your contract must state the minimum, normally available, maximum and advertised download and upload speeds.

## If you're in the National Broadband Plan area

National Broadband Ireland (NBI) designs, builds and runs the network for the National Broadband Plan. Search [NBI's map](https://nbi.ie/map/) by Eircode or address to see whether your home is in the rollout and when it's expected to be ready for connection. The dates can change, and NBI updates them regularly.

NBI doesn't sell broadband to homes. To get connected:

1. If your area isn't ready yet, you can pre-order; the pre-order becomes an order automatically when the area is ready.
2. Otherwise, choose a provider from NBI's [Where to buy](https://nbi.ie/where-can-i-buy-customer-contribution/) list and order from it.
3. NBI then contacts you to confirm an installation appointment.

If connecting your home needs significantly more work than usual, such as new poles or ducting, NBI asks you for a contribution towards the cost. Its website doesn't give an amount.

## If you're not in the plan and get 30Mb or less

1. Ask your current provider why your speeds are slow.
2. Ask other providers whether they can serve your home.
3. If none can give you more than 30Mb, contact the National Broadband Plan division of the Department of Culture, Communications and Sport through its web form.

## Getting connected

Before you change your connection, ComReg suggests asking your provider:

- which technology it will use, such as fibre or fixed wireless
- what will change with your home phone, and tell it if you're keeping your number
- what happens in a power cut: a digital phone may only work then if a battery backup powers your modem
- whether medical, panic, security or fire alarms, or schemes like the Seniors Alert Scheme, will be affected
- whether a technician needs to visit and from which company, whether there's an extra cost, and whether the work is standard or needs extra works

The technician may be from a different company to your provider. They'll check where the equipment goes inside and outside, whether the line comes overhead or underground, anything that could get in the way, such as tree branches near poles or a driveway that needs digging, and whether extra poles or civil works are needed.

To prepare:

- Tell your provider and the technician about any critical medical alarms.
- If you rent, get your landlord's permission.
- Decide where the line should come in: a hole may need to be drilled through a wall, so check what's on the other side.
- Pick a central spot with a power socket for the modem and Wi-Fi.

On the day, it's best for you or someone you authorise to be there. Agree how long the work will take, the cable route, where the wires come in and where the connection box goes, and any downtime, and test your services and devices once it's done.
