If your mobile signal is poor at home, ComReg's advice is to check which network gives the best coverage where you live, then turn on WiFi Calling if your network and phone support it, and lastly consider a mobile repeater. Repeaters are legal and need no licence, but only ones that meet ComReg's conditions and carry the CE mark: cheap boosters that don't are illegal.
{: .answer}

Insulation, double and triple glazing and thick walls can all weaken the signal inside, so you can have a signal outside your home and none inside.
{: .answer}

{% include key-points.html %}

## Why the signal can be worse indoors

ComReg lists insulation, double and triple glazed windows, metal-framed windows and thick walls. Its research found that the materials used to make homes better insulated often limit mobile coverage. Three says this applies to newer homes and other buildings, and that you might need to move to another part of the building, or go outside, for a better signal.

Outdoors, coverage depends on hills and valleys, nearby trees, how far you are from a mast, how many masts your network has in the area, how many people are using it at the same time, and whether you're on 4G or 5G.

Your phone matters too. ComReg says modern phones outperform older, basic models, and its testing found that some phones have better antennas than others.

## 1. Check which network covers your home best

ComReg's free outdoor coverage map shows the mobile providers' predicted 2G, 3G, 4G and 5G coverage for an address or Eircode. Find it at comreg.ie/coveragemap, or use ComReg's free app for iPhone and Android, which checks coverage where you are.

The map predicts coverage outdoors. Indoors, insulation and thick walls can make it weaker. ComReg's Siteviewer tool, on its website, shows where each network's masts are.

If another network is better where you live, you can switch and keep your number: see [How to keep your number when you switch mobile network]({{ '/phones/keep-your-number/' | relative_url }}).

## 2. Turn on WiFi Calling

ComReg's next step is WiFi Calling, if your network and phone support it. It lets you make and receive calls over a Wi-Fi connection, such as your home broadband, and it works on phones that support it, once you turn it on. eir offers it to its customers in areas its mobile signal doesn't reach.

For which networks offer it, and whether texts work over it too, see [Which Irish networks support WiFi Calling?]({{ '/phones/wifi-calling-networks/' | relative_url }}). If your Wi-Fi doesn't reach every room, see [How to fix Wi-Fi dead spots at home]({{ '/broadband/wifi-dead-spots/' | relative_url }}).

## 3. Rule out your phone and SIM

eir's checks for a coverage problem:

- Make sure mobile data is on and you haven't used up your data allowance.
- Restart the phone, or turn flight mode on and off.
- Try your SIM in another phone. If it works there, the problem is likely your phone. If it doesn't, it could be the SIM (eir gives new SIMs in its stores).

Three adds that pending software updates or a full phone storage may affect your service, and that a phone that doesn't support 4G or 5G won't get those speeds.

## 4. Consider a mobile repeater

A repeater brings an outdoor signal inside. It usually has three parts: an outside antenna, like a TV aerial; an inside antenna; and the repeater box, like a Wi-Fi modem. It can improve calls, texts and data indoors.

**Will it work for you?** If you have some signal outside but none inside, a repeater can amplify the outside signal indoors. If you have no signal outside either, a highly directional antenna pointed at a mast might still pick one up: ComReg says to ask an installer.

**Is it legal?** Yes, with no licence or registration, as long as it meets ComReg's technical conditions. These were updated in 2026 (S.I. No. 86 of 2026) and cover repeaters used indoors and in vehicles. The repeater must meet the EU's Radio Equipment Directive, carry the CE mark and come with a Declaration of Conformity. Among the conditions, it must control its own power so it doesn't interfere with the network's masts, and switch itself off if it can't.

**Where to buy and who fits it.** ComReg publishes a list of manufacturers whose repeaters meet its conditions, and a list of suppliers and installers, on its [mobile phone repeaters page](https://www.comreg.ie/advice-information/mobile/get-the-most-out-of-your-mobile-service/mobile-phone-repeaters/). It warns you to be wary of online marketplaces, as their devices may not comply. Anyone can fit a repeater, but it means putting an antenna outside, possibly with ladders and power tools, and knowing where your nearest mast is. ComReg strongly recommends a professional installer.

**Cost and networks.** The price depends on what you want covered: calls and texts, data, or both. A multi-operator repeater can handle calls on different networks at the same time, and there's no limit on the number of phones. If only one network is weak in your home, a single-operator repeater may be more convenient.

**Interference.** ComReg says a compliant repeater won't interfere with your Wi-Fi, TV, or wireless cameras, doorbells or gates, because they use different frequencies.

## Why cheap boosters are illegal

Devices sold as boosters and as repeaters both amplify mobile signals, but only some meet ComReg's conditions. ComReg says boosters are usually cheap amplifiers with no built-in protection against interfering with mobile networks, and most don't meet the Radio Equipment Directive or carry the CE mark. That interference can block or drop calls for other people in the area.

A device that doesn't comply is illegal to sell or use in Ireland. ComReg will confiscate it and users may be prosecuted: anyone who has one can be fined up to €5,000, or up to €250,000 on conviction on indictment. ComReg sends take-down notices to online marketplaces selling these devices and publishes a list of websites that sell non-compliant repeaters.

## If your signal gets worse

You can complain to your network. Networks must respond to a complaint within 10 working days; if yours doesn't, ComReg may take it up with the network for you.
