If a phone, laptop or other gadget turns out to be faulty within 30 days of getting it, you can hand it back for a full refund. After that, the seller has to repair or replace it, and give you a refund or money off if that doesn't work, for up to six years.
{: .answer}

It's the shop you bought from that has to put it right, not the manufacturer, and a warranty or store policy can't take these rights away. Here's how it works, and what to do if the shop says no.
{: .answer}

## In the first 30 days: your money back

If you tell the seller about the fault within 30 days of buying it in a shop or having it delivered, you can reject it and get a full refund. You don't have to accept a repair or a replacement instead, though you can choose one if you'd rather. If the item had to be installed, the 30 days start when the installation is finished.

The 30 days is a deadline for telling the seller, so get in touch as soon as you notice the fault.

The seller pays to get the item back. If you pay the postage yourself, they must pay you back, so keep your proof of postage. Your refund is due within 14 days of the seller getting the item back.

## After 30 days: a repair or a replacement

After the first 30 days, you choose whether you'd like the item repaired or replaced. The seller can only refuse your choice if it's impossible (for example, a replacement that's no longer available) or would cost far more than the other option.

A repair must be free, done within a reasonable time and without major hassle for you. You don't have to take a refurbished replacement unless you ask for one.

If the same fault keeps coming back, you don't have to keep accepting repairs once you've lost confidence they'll work. You can ask for a replacement instead, or have it repaired elsewhere and claim the cost, as long as you tell the seller first.

## When you can still get a refund, or money off

You can get a full refund by handing the item back, or keep it and get a price reduction that reflects the fault, if a repair or replacement:

- isn't possible, or would cost the seller too much
- isn't done within a reasonable time, or without significant inconvenience to you

For a serious fault you can go straight to a refund without accepting a repair. That covers a fault that's a safety risk, an item you can't use where a repair can't be relied on to fix it, or a seller you no longer have confidence in.

## It's the shop's job, not the manufacturer's

The business that sold you the item is responsible for faults. If a shop tells you to contact the manufacturer, remind it that under the Consumer Rights Act 2022 the seller is responsible.

A guarantee or warranty is extra, on top of these rights. It can't replace them, and neither can a shop's own returns policy. You don't have to accept a credit note or voucher for a faulty item, and sale items carry the same rights.

## Out of warranty? You may still have up to six years

In Ireland, you can seek a remedy for a faulty item for up to six years from when you received it, even if the warranty has run out.

For the first 12 months, the fault is presumed to have been there from the start, so it's up to the seller to show otherwise. After 12 months, it's up to you to show the fault was there when you got it. That's one more reason to report a fault as soon as you notice it.

## What isn't covered

These rights cover faults, not damage. Problems caused by misuse, accidental damage or normal wear and tear aren't covered. Items bought before 29 November 2022 come under the older law, so the rules above may not apply to them.

## How to make a claim

1. **Find your proof of purchase.** A receipt is easiest, but a card statement or delivery docket also works. For a gift, use the gift receipt or ask the person who bought it.
2. **Contact the seller, in writing if you can.** Describe the fault and say how you want it put right: a refund, a repair or a replacement. The CCPC has free [letter templates](https://ccpc.ie/consumer-advice/faulty-products-and-goods/repairs-and-replacements).
3. **Keep proof of postage** if you send the item back, in case there's a dispute about when it arrived.
4. **Paying in instalments?** You can hold back an amount that reflects the item's lower value until it's sorted, as long as you tell the seller.

The CCPC's [faulty goods tool](https://www.ccpc.ie/consumer-advice/faulty-products-and-goods) walks you through your options for your own situation.

## If the shop says no

If you can't settle it with the seller, the [small claims procedure](https://courts.ie/guides/about-small-claim) lets you claim up to €2,000 without a solicitor. It costs €25, which you can't get back, both you and the seller must be in Ireland, and you must claim within six years.

A small claims registrar contacts both sides first and tries to settle the claim before it goes to court.

If the shop has gone into liquidation, contact the liquidator. You'll be treated as an unsecured creditor, which means you're likely to be low on the list to be paid.

## Bought from another EU country, the UK or further away?

Everything above applies when you buy from a business in Ireland. If you bought from a business in another EU country, you're entitled to a repair or replacement for up to two years, and European Consumer Centre Ireland may be able to help.

If you buy from outside the EU, including the UK, the US and China, you have fewer protections and can't use the small claims procedure. Check where a seller is based before you buy: a .ie web address doesn't prove it's Irish. If a seller outside the EU won't help and you paid by card, you may be able to ask your bank for a chargeback; ask early, as card-scheme time limits are usually 90 to 120 days.
