If you buy a refurbished phone or laptop from a business in Ireland, you have similar rights to buying new: it must be fit for its normal use and match its description, and its quality should reflect the price you paid.
{: .answer}

Buy online and you can usually change your mind within 14 days of getting it. Buy from a private seller and it's buyer beware, so check the item carefully before you pay.
{: .answer}

{% include key-points.html %}

## Your rights when you buy from a business

Consumer law protects you when you buy from a business, including a sole trader. A refurbished item must be fit for the purpose it's normally used for, match what the seller described or showed you, and suit any purpose the seller knew you were buying it for.

You can't expect it to be as good as new, but the quality should reflect the price. These rights don't cover a fault, or wear and tear, that was pointed out to you before you bought it.

If something goes wrong, you can get a full refund within 30 days, and a repair, replacement or refund for up to six years. For how to claim, see [Your rights when tech is faulty in Ireland]({{ '/buying/faulty-tech-rights/' | relative_url }}).

On online marketplaces, the site must show you whether the seller is a business or a private person. A business pretending to be a private seller is breaking the law.

## Changing your mind

If you buy in a shop, you have no legal right to a refund just because you changed your mind, though some shops offer one. Buy online, by phone or by mail order and, in most cases, you can cancel for any reason within 14 days of receiving it. The CCPC says you then have a further 14 days to send it back, and you get a full refund, including standard delivery.

When the CCPC and other European regulators checked 356 second-hand websites in 2025, 40% didn't tell buyers about this right, and 45% didn't correctly explain the right to return faulty goods.

## Warranties from refurbished sellers

Some sellers add their own warranty. For example:

- **Apple's** Certified Refurbished products come with its standard one-year limited warranty and free delivery and returns, and refurbished iPhones get a new battery and outer shell.
- **Three's** refurbished phones are refurbished in Ireland, come with a one-year warranty, and have original parts and batteries above 80% health.

A warranty is extra. It doesn't replace or change your rights under Irish law, and the seller should still repair, replace or refund a faulty item after the warranty runs out.

## What to check before you buy

The CCPC's advice is to examine the item for faults, imperfections and wear and tear, and to ask the seller about its history and condition. Buying online? Read the return policy, and ask whether the seller tests or certifies its refurbished devices and what it checks.

For an iPhone, Apple's checks include:

- **Activation Lock:** if you see iPhone Locked to Owner, don't buy it, or return it if you bought it online.
- **Battery:** Settings > Battery > Battery Health & Charging shows the battery's maximum capacity.
- **Replaced parts:** on some models, Settings > General > About shows a Parts and Service History section.
- **Network lock:** in Settings > General > About, "No SIM restrictions" next to Network Provider Lock means it's unlocked.
- **Then test** the buttons, cameras, speakers, microphone and display.

If a refurbished laptop runs Windows 10, see [Windows 10 support has ended: your options in Ireland]({{ '/computers/windows-10-end-of-support/' | relative_url }}). Buying from a seller in the UK? See [Buying tech from a UK shop: VAT, customs and returns]({{ '/buying/uk-shop-vat-customs/' | relative_url }}).

## Buying from a private seller

Consumer law applies only when you buy from a business. Buy from another person, for example through a second-hand website or app, and it's buyer beware: it's up to you to check the item before you buy.

For an iPhone, Apple's advice is to ask the seller to erase it so you can test it, and, if you see iPhone Locked to Owner, to ask them to enter their password to turn off Activation Lock.
